\subsection{Real-data Oaxaca--Blinder decomposition and drawing conclusions} 
\label{sec:ob_real}

In general, data analysts will have access to real data for each of the two groups. We assume they fit a linear model
to data in each group separately. Then they can apply \cref{eq:OB_H_decomp,eq:OB_K_decomp} with the fitted intercept and 
slope values for each $\alpha, \beta$ and the empirical mean covariate vectors for each $\mu$.
In our own experiments below, we use ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression in each group.
\textcolor{red}{double check since i didnt see it in the main text: it is OLS, right?}

It is common to draw substantive conclusions based on the signs of the empirical explained and unexplained components, often coupled with
statistical significance \textcolor{red}{cite}. For instance, consider the example with covariate BMI and outcome SBP. 
Suppose city $H$ has lower SBP ($\Delta_Y < 0$), and suppose we take $H$ as the reference. 
If the OBD explained component has a negative sign, we might conclude that city $H$ can attribute
its lower SBP in part to its lower average BMI. In real data, we might additionally require that the observed sign be statistically significant in order to form a conclusion;
without significance, we might
instead hesitate to conclude BMI is driving any difference in SBP.
Analogous to the explained component, if the OBD unexplained component has a negative sign, we might attribute city $H$'s lower
SBP in part to institutional factors.
