\section{Theory}
\label{app:theory}

In this section, we present the proof of \cref{thm:ordering_sensitivity} and an example showing that the difference between the two decompositions can be made arbitrarily large even when the mean gap is fixed.


\subsection{Proof of \cref{thm:ordering_sensitivity}}
\label{sec:sign_flip_proof}
\begin{proof}
For a sign flip to occur, it must be that the unexplained components have different signs. That is:
\begin{equation}
	\sign( \mu_H^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha) \neq \sign( \mu_K^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha)\end{equation}
First, consider the case where $\sign \mu_K^T \Delta\beta = \sign \mu_H^T \Delta\beta$.
In this case, the addition of $\Delta\alpha$ must be small enough to flip one of the signs of the $\mu_g^T \Delta\beta$ but so large that it flips the other.
As we have assumed $\mu_K^T \Delta\beta \neq \mu_H^T \Delta\beta$, one of the $\mu_g^T \Delta\beta$ will be lesser in magnitude; without loss of generality, suppose this is $g = H$.
That is, $\abs{\mu_H^T \Delta\beta} < \abs{\mu_K^T \Delta\beta}$.
Now, we have also assumed that $\sign \Delta\alpha \neq \sign \mu_K^T \Delta\beta$ and $\abs{\mu_H^T \Delta\beta} < \abs{\Delta\alpha} < \abs{\mu_K^T \Delta\beta}$.
We conclude that $\sign( \mu_H^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha) \neq \sign( \mu_K^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha)$.

Next, consider the case where $\sign \mu_K^T \Delta\beta \neq \sign \mu_H^T \Delta\beta$.
In this case, the addition of $\Delta\alpha$ to the $\mu_g^T \Delta\beta$ must be small enough such that it does not change the sign of either one.
Without loss of generality, suppose that $\sign \Delta\alpha \neq \sign \mu_H^T \Delta\beta$.
Then the assumed condition $\abs{\mu_H^T \Delta\beta} > \abs{\Delta\alpha}$ is enough to ensure that $\sign(\mu_H^T \Delta\beta) = \sign(\mu_H^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha) \neq \sign(\mu_K^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Unbounded sensitivity at fixed mean gap}
 \label{ex:unbounded}
In this section we present a toy example where we can have arbitrarily large flip magnitudes. Consider the following example in dimension \( 1 \), where we assume we have a parameter \( L > 0 \) which controls the magnitude of the covariate shift, and \( \varepsilon > 0 \) which controls the deviation in slope coefficients. We assume the mean vector is given by \( (\mu_K, \mu_H) = (0, L) \), and the slope parameters deviate symmetrically from \( \frac{1}{L} \), so that \( \beta_H = \frac{1}{L} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \), \( \beta_K = \frac{1}{L} - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \). Then, the explained and unexplained components under both reference groups are:
\begin{align*}
    \EHOB &= \Delta\mu \cdot \beta_H = L\left(\frac{1}{L} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right) = 1 + \frac{L\varepsilon}{2}, \quad \UH = \mu_K \cdot \Delta\beta = -\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}, \\
    \EKOB &= \Delta\mu \cdot \beta_K = L\left(\frac{1}{L} - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right) = 1 - \frac{L\varepsilon}{2}, \quad \UK = \mu_H \cdot \Delta\beta = +\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}.
\end{align*}

Hence, the difference between the two decompositions is \( \EHOB - \EKOB = L\varepsilon \), which can be made arbitrarily large as \( L \to \infty \), even though the total mean gap is always fixed at \( \Delta_Y = 1 \). Similarly, the unexplained components differ by \( \UK - \UH = L\varepsilon \).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccccc}
\toprule
\(L\) & \(\varepsilon\) 
& \(\EHOB\) & \(\EKOB\) 
& \(\UH\) & \(\UK\) 
& Flip? \\
\midrule
20    & 0.1  & \(2\)     & \(0\)      & \(-1\)    & \(1\)     & \checkmark \\
200   & 0.1  & \(11\)    & \(-9\)     & \(-10\)   & \(10\)    & \checkmark \\
1000  & 0.1  & \(51\)    & \(-49\)    & \(-50\)   & \(50\)    & \checkmark \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Note that the sign flip is symmetric by construction, but the magnitude of the discrepancy grows linearly in \( L \), highlighting that the attribution gap between reference groups can be stretched arbitrarily far.

\subsection{Proportion of Parameter Space that Produces Sign Flips}
\label{app:sign_flip_volume}

We now compute the volume of the parameter space that can produce sign flips in the explained and unexplained components of the OBD.
To simplify calculations, we assume the variables are scaled so that \( \E_K[X] = 0 \) and \( \E_H[X] = 1 \).
We can achieve this by transforming the covariates via \( \tilde{X} = (X - \mu_K) / (\mu_H - \mu_K) \), which is permissible as long as \( \mu_H \neq \mu_K \) elementwise. 
We view this without loss of generality since \( \mu_H \neq \mu_K \) elementwise holds almost surely when the covariates are drawn from an atomless distributions.
With this rescaling \( \Delta \mu = \mathbf{1} \), where \( \mathbf{1} \) is a vector of ones.

We start with the explained component.
Recall that the covariates, \( X \), are $d$-dimensional.
After rescaling, \cref{eq:sign_flip_condition_explained_neq} simplifies to 
\[ \sign\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\top \beta_{H}\right) \neq \sign\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\top \beta_{K}\right). \]
A Uniform(-M, M) distribution on \( \beta_H \) and \( \beta_K \) translates to distributions on \( \mathbf{1}^\top \beta_{H} \) and \( \mathbf{1}^\top \beta_{K} \) that are identical and symmetric around zero.
As such, the signs of \( \mathbf{1}^\top \beta_{H} \) and \( \mathbf{1}^\top \beta_{H} \) will disagree in 50\% of the parameter space.

Now we turn to the unexplained component.
For the rescaled covariates, the conditions in \cref{alg:decision_tree} simplify, eliminating several of its branches.
In particular, the unexplained component flips sign if and only if \( \mathbf{1}^T \Delta \beta \neq 0 \), \( \sign (\Delta \alpha) \neq \sign (\mathbf{1}^\top \Delta \beta) \), and \( | \mathbf{1}^T \Delta \beta | > |\Delta \alpha| \).
The first condition holds almost surely, so we can focus on the latter two.
To do so, we will determine the distributions of  \( \Delta \alpha \) and \( \mathbf{1}^\top \Delta \beta \).

The sum of \( n \) Uniform(0,1) random variables is sometimes called the Irwin-Hall(\(n\)) distribution.%
\footnote{For \(n = 2\) this is a familiar triangular distribution.}
When we place a Uniform(-M, M) measure on the \( \alpha \)s and \( \beta \)s, there is a straightforward relationship between \( \Delta \alpha \), \( \mathbf{1}^\top \Delta \beta \), and appropriately defined Irwin-Hall random variables.
The difference \( \Delta \alpha \) is the sum of two Uniform(-M, M) variables, which is equivalent to an Irwin-Hall($2$) distribution, rescaled to the interval $[-2M, 2M]$.
Similarly, \( \mathbf{1}^T \Delta \beta \) is the sum of \( 2 d \) Uniform(-M, M) variables, and its distribution is an Irwin-Hall($2 d$) distribution, rescaled to the domain \( -2dM, 2dM \).

We can use this correspondence to translate the event 
\[ | 1^T \Delta \beta | > |\Delta \alpha| \cup \sign (\Delta \alpha) \neq \sign (1^T \Delta \beta) \]
into an equivalent event in terms of two independent Irwin-Hall random variables.
In particular, let \( I_2 \sim \text{Irwin-Hall}(2) \) and \( J_{2d} \sim \text{Irwin-Hall}(2d) \) be independent random variables.
The above event is equivalent to 
\[ (I_2 > 1 \cap J_{2d} \in [-2d, \frac{2dM - I_2}{2M}]) \cup (I_2 < 1 \cap J_{2d} \in [\frac{I_2 + 2dM}{2M}, 2d]).  \]
That is, unexplained component sign flips have the same volume as the region where \( I_2 \) falls above its median and \( J_{2d} \) falls far in its left tail, or vice versa.
Because both \( I_2 \) and \( J_{2d} \) are symmetric, this volume is equal to twice the probability that \( I_2 \) is above its median and \( J_{2d} \) is sufficiently small.
That is:
\[ Pr(| 1^T \Delta \beta | > |\Delta \alpha|) = 2 Pr(I_2 > 1 \cap J_{2d} < \frac{2dM - I_2}{2M}). \] 

We can calculate this exactly for small \( d \) by using the Irwin-Hall(n) cumulative distribution function,
\[ F(x) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x \rfloor} (-1)^k {n \choose k} (x - k)^n. \]
For larger \( d \), the combinatorial terms in the CDF become difficult to evaluate.
In those cases the central limit theorem allows us to approximate an Irwin-Hall(2d) distribution with a \( N(d, \frac{d}{6})\) distribution.
Finally, as \( d \to \infty \), sign flips have a volume equal to
\[ 2 Pr(I_2 > 1) Pr(J_{2d} < d | I_2 > 1).  \] 
Since the median of \( I_2 \) is 1 and the median of \( J_{2d} \) is 2, both probabilities are equal to \( \frac{1}{2} \), and the corresponding volume is therefore \( 0.5 \).

\subsection{Relationship Between Regression Parameters and Omitted Variables}
\label{app:omitted_variables}

We start by noting that the OBD is based on a linear model for the expected value of the outcome in terms of the covariates:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:y_on_x}    
    \E_g[Y] = \alpha_g + \E_g[X]^\top \beta_g.
\end{align}
Let \( Z \in \mathbb{R}^{d'} \) be a vector of \textit{omitted covariates} that are not recorded in an analyst's dataset.

We can rewrite the expected value of the outcome using both \( X \) and \( Z \) as
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:y_on_x_z}
    \E_g[Y] = \omega_g + \E_g[X]^\top \theta_g + \E_g[Z]^\top \gamma_g.
\end{align}
We can also express the relationship between the omitted and observed covariates as 
\begin{align}
   \label{eq:z_on_x}
   \E_g[Z] &= \zeta_g + \Psi_g \E_g[X],
\end{align}
where \( \Psi_g \in \mathbb{R}^{d' \times d} \).
Plugging \cref{eq:z_on_x} into \cref{eq:y_on_x_z} and rearranging terms gives
\[ \E_g[Y] = \omega_g + \zeta_g^\top \gamma_g + \E_g[X]^\top (\theta_g + \Psi_g^\top \gamma_g).\]
By comparing this to \cref{eq:y_on_x}, we obtain
\begin{align*}    
   &\alpha_g = \omega_g + \zeta_g^\top \gamma_g,
   &\beta_g  = \theta_g + \Psi_g^\top \gamma_g.
\end{align*}
Similarly,
\begin{align*}
  &\Delta \alpha = \Delta \omega + \Delta \zeta^\top \Delta \gamma,
  &\Delta \beta  = \Delta \theta + \Delta \Psi^\top \Delta \gamma,
\end{align*}
where \( \Delta \) takes the \( H - K \) difference in the corresponding parameter. 
For example \( \Delta \omega = \omega_H - \omega_K \).
