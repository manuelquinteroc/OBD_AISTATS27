\section{Oaxaca--Blinder decomposition (OBD)} 
\label{sec:ob}

We now formalize the discussion above tailored to the OBD.
Let \( X \in \mathbb{R}^d \) be a vector of covariates and \( Y \in \mathbb{R} \) the outcome of interest. 
The OBD assumes the outcome-generating mechanism is linear in the covariates, with conditional expectation \( \mathbb{E}_g[Y \mid X] = \alpha_g + X^\top \beta_g,\) where \( \alpha_g \in \mathbb{R} \) is the intercept and \( \beta_g \in \mathbb{R}^d \) the slope vector for group \( g \in \{H, K\} \). 

The OBD can be interpreted as a specific implementation of the two counterfactual decompositions in \cref{sec:counterfactual_example}.
Under the linear model assumption, the counterfactual means can be expressed as:
\[
    \E\big[Y_{g}^{C(g')}\big] = \E_{g}[X]^\top \beta_{g'} + \alpha_{g'}, \quad \{g, g'\} = \{H, K\}, g \ne g'.
\]
Hence, OBD is the rewriting of \cref{eq:OB_H_Counterfactual,eq:OB_K_Counterfactual} as
\begin{align}
    \Delta_Y &:= \E_H[Y] - \E_K[Y] \nonumber \\[2pt]
    \label{eq:OB_H_decomp}
    &= \underbrace{(\mu_H - \mu_K)^\top \beta_H}_{E^{(H)}} 
     + \underbrace{\mu_K^\top(\beta_H - \beta_K) + (\alpha_H - \alpha_K)}_{U^{(H)}} \\[2pt]
    \label{eq:OB_K_decomp}
    &= \underbrace{\mu_H^\top(\beta_H - \beta_K) + (\alpha_H - \alpha_K)}_{U^{(K)}} 
     + \underbrace{(\mu_H - \mu_K)^\top \beta_K}_{E^{(K)}}.
\end{align}

Where \( \mu_g := \mathbb{E}_g[X] \) is the mean covariate vector in group \( g \). 
%and \( g' \) denotes the group not used as reference (i.e., \( \{g, g'\} = \{H, K\} \) with \( g \ne g' \)).
The OBD attributes the mean outcome gap \( \Delta_Y \) to the explained component (\(E^{(K)}\) or \(E^{(H)}\)), reflecting differences in the distribution of \( X \), and to the unexplained component (\(U^{(K)}\) or \(U^{(H)}\)), reflecting differences in the conditional distribution of \( Y \mid X \).
As can be clearly seen from \cref{eq:OB_H_decomp,eq:OB_K_decomp}, \(E^{(H)}\) is generally different from \(E^{(K)}\), unless \(\beta_H = \beta_K\), and similarly for \(U^{(H)}\) and \(U^{(K)}\).
As noted in \cref{sec:counterfactual_example}, the decomposition clearly depends on the chosen counterfactual distribution.
Letting
\[
    \Delta \mu := \mu_H - \mu_K, \quad
    \Delta \beta := \beta_H - \beta_K, \quad
    \Delta \alpha := \alpha_H - \alpha_K,
\]
we can write the two possible decompositions, one for each choice of reference group, as:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:ob_decomp_ref_group}
    \Delta_Y &= \underbrace{\Delta \mu^\top \beta_H}_{=:\EHOB} + \underbrace{\mu_K^\top \Delta \beta + \Delta \alpha}_{=:\UH} \nonumber \\
             &= \underbrace{\Delta \mu^\top \beta_K}_{=:\EKOB} + \underbrace{\mu_H^\top \Delta \beta + \Delta \alpha}_{=:\UK}.
\end{align}
Our contribution is to show that the differences between the two decompositions can be so substantial that it is not clear either one is meaningful in real-world applications.
