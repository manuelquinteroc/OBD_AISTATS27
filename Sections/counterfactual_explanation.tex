\subsection{Difference in means problem: A counterfactual exercise} 
\label{sec:counterfactual_example}

As a first step toward describing the OBD, we start by illustrating the index-number problem by framing it as a counterfactual exercise as in \citet{fortinLemieuxFirpo2011}. 

We consider an observed outcome $Y \in \mathbb{R}$ and covariates $X$ in some space $\mathcal{X}$. We consider two groups, $H$ and $K$. We let $\mathbb{P}_g$ denote the distribution of $(X,Y)$ for members of group $g$. We suppose we observe a gap in the mean outcomes, $\E_H[Y]$ and $\E_K[Y]$, and would like to understand to what extent the gap is due to differences in covariates between the two groups. 
For the moment, we assume we have access to population-level quantities, including all expectations of interest.

To analyze this gap, we might construct a counterfactual world driven only by differences in covariates.
Consider again our example, where $Y$ represents systolic blood pressure, and $X$ represents body mass index. Suppose we think the conditional distribution $Y|X$ is attributable to city-specific public health and medical care at each BMI level.
Then we might ask:
(A) \emph{How much would city $H$'s mean SBP change if city $H$ kept its current health care but had the BMI distribution of city $K$?}
But we notice this question starts from City $H$ as a reference. We could instead start from City $K$ and ask:
(B) \emph{How much would city $K$'s mean SBP change if city $K$ kept its current health care but had the BMI distribution of city $H$?}

Analogously, the gender wage gap forms a classic example \citep{oaxaca1973, blinder1973} with $Y$ as wages and $X$ as education. We might ask:
(A) \emph{How much would women's ($H$) mean wages change if women kept their current remuneration relationship with education but had the educational distribution of men ($K$)?}
or instead 
(B) \emph{How much would men's ($K$) mean wages change if men kept their current remuneration relationship with education but had the educational distribution of women ($H$)?}

More generally, we might ask:
(A) \emph{How much would Group $H$'s mean outcome ($Y$) change if group $H$ kept its outcome--covariate relationship ($Y|X$) but had the covariate ($X$) distribution of group $K$?}
or 
(B) \emph{How much would Group $K$'s mean outcome ($Y$) change if group $K$ kept its outcome--covariate relationship ($Y|X$) but had the covariate ($X$) distribution of group $H$?}

In the general case, we can compute the mean of a counterfactual $Y$ that has group $H$'s outcome--covariate relationship ($Y|X$) and group $K$'s covariate ($X$) distribution as follows. In particular, by using
the law of total expectation, we have
$\E_K [ \E_H  [ Y | X ] ]$, with an analogous formula when the groups are reversed.

Then the difference in mean outcomes between the two groups can be expressed as the distance between each group's mean
and the chosen counterfactual mean. The decomposition corresponding to question (A) above is:
%
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:OB_H_Counterfactual}
    &\E_H[Y] - \E_K[Y] = \\ 
    &\underbrace{\Big(\E_H[Y] - \E_K [ \E_H  [ Y | X ] ] \Big)}_{E^{(H)}} \nonumber 
     + \underbrace{\Big(\E_K [ \E_H  [ Y | X ] ] - \E_K[Y]\Big)}_{U^{(H)} }.
\end{align} 
Alternatively, the decomposition corresponding to question (B) above is:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:OB_K_Counterfactual}
    &\E_H[Y] - \E_K[Y]= \\
    &\underbrace{\Big(\E_H[Y] - \E_H [ \E_K  [ Y | X ] ] \Big)}_{U^{(K)}} \nonumber 
     + \underbrace{\Big(\E_H [ \E_K  [ Y | X ] ] - \E_K[Y]\Big)}_{E^{(K)}}.
\end{align}
%
Traditionally the term corresponding to changing the covariate distribution is called the
``explained component'' since this part of the difference is explained by the observed
covariates \textcolor{red}{cite}. The remaining term, corresponding to changing the conditional $Y|X$ distribution,
is called the ``unexplained component'' since it is not explained by the observed covariates. We use the 
notation $E$ and $U$ to correspond to these components, respectively, with superscript denoting 
the reference group choice.\textcolor{red}{check: is our use of the word reference here consistent with any established usage? in particular, does it get used in the symmetric way?}

We might interpret $E$ and $U$ as the respective contributions of the covariates and outcome--covariate relationship to the 
difference in outcome means between groups $H$ and $K$. The \textbf{index number problem}, then, represents the observation
that in general we might obtain different results by starting from $H$ or $K$ as the reference.

\textbf{A conceptual resolution?}
Before proceeding, we observe that there is not an immediate and general conceptual resolution of the index number problem.
It is not clear to us in the SBP--BMI example above that one direction is more natural --- or even that the two
directions represent clearly distinguishable policy questions.
There may be resolutions in particular areas of study.
Many papers on the gender wage gap seem to favor taking men as the reference \textcolor{red}{cite}; this tendency might suggest a consensus.
However, we also observe the existence of published work that takes women as the reference \textcolor{red}{cite}.
