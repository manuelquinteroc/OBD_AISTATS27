\section{Introduction}
As a motivating example, suppose a data scientist at a hospital notices that mortality rates are noticeably higher in patient group $H$ than in patient group $K$.
She knows the medical literature points to many risk factors ($X$), such as high blood pressure, and patient mortality ($Y$).
The observed differences in mortality could be due to differences in risk factors between the two groups (distribution of $X$) or to differences in how well the hospital manages these risk factors (distribution of $Y|X$).
If she can figure out how much each of these factors is driving the difference in mortality rates, the hospital may be able to change its policy to improve patient care.
Before committing to a potentially costly causal analysis, can she use the available information to develop hypotheses for what drives the difference in mortality?

The modern explainability literature in machine learning (ML) provides a number of methods for attributing outcome behavior to covariates \citep{Dwivedi2023}. These include Shapley values \citep{Shapley1953, Lundberg2017}, Functional ANOVA (FANOVA) \citep{stone1994, hooker2004discovering, hookerGeneralizedFunctionalANOVA2007, lengerich2020purifying}, Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) \citep{apley2020}, and partial dependence plots (PDP) \citep{friedman2001greedy}. However, these methods all focus on decompositions within a single group, not on attributing differences across groups. By contrast, the Oaxaca--Blinder decomposition (OBD) exactly attributes disparities between groups to $X$ and $Y|X$. The OBD was traditionally developed only for linear models \citep{oaxaca1973,blinder1973} and remains widely used in this form to the present day --- e.g., to analyze gender wage gaps \citep{paradaContzen2025}, educational attainment differences \citep{barhaim2023}, and diabetes health disparities \citep{cartwright2021}. Moreover, recent work suggests the OBD might be applied in conjunction with flexible ML decompositions \citep{bach2024heterogeneity,quintas2024multiply,quintero2025}.

Since the original paper \citep{oaxaca1973}, it has been well known that the OBD requires choosing one of the two groups (City $K$ or City $H$ in our example) as a reference, and the numerical result returned by the OBD can change with the choice of reference; this dependence on the reference choice is sometimes known as the ``index-number problem.''
%And generalizations of the OBD to non-linear models of $Y \mid X$ do not seem to escape this issue -- e.g., \textcolor{red}{TODO: cite our workshop paper} consider one fixed reference group.
Several responses to this asymmetry occur in the OBD literature. 
Some argue that, in certain cases,
a single reference group makes the most sense to use in context; e.g. \citet{dinardoFortinLemieux1996} take this approach in a study of the effects of unionization and the minimum wage on economic inequality.
%Or in other cases, authors construct their own new reference group by pooling across the original two groups \citep{neumark1988,oaxacaRansom1994}.
Some effectively construct their own new reference group by aggregating in various forms across the original two groups \citep{neumark1988,oaxacaRansom1994,reimers1983,cotton1988,sharafRashad2016,rahimiNazari2021,Allen2022}.

However, in many applications, there will be no clear choice of reference group. Moreover, there is no consensus on the appropriate resolution.
Some papers report results for both references \citep{ONeill2006,fortinLemieuxFirpo2011,sharafRashad2016,rahimiNazari2021}. 
Some report results for just one reference, with no argument for excluding the other reference \citep{zhang_2019, ayubiShahbaziKhazaei2024,paradaContzen2025,singleton2016,lamJamiesonMittinty2021, alHanawiNjagi2022}.

A priori, it is possible that, despite the index number problem, both references always yield the same substantive conclusions;
e.g., in our example, both references might say the mortality difference between patient groups is driven in part by risk factors and just differ in how much.
In this case, all of the approaches above should at least yield the same general conclusions.
However, if the choice of reference could yield different substantive conclusions, the distinction in approaches \emph{would} matter;
e.g., suppose one reference tells us that 
the mortality difference is driven by a difference in risk factors and the other says it is not.
While the index-number problem states that the two references can give different numerical answers, we are not aware of a systematic investigation
into whether the two different references can yield different \emph{substantive} conclusions. We fill this gap.

In what remains, we start by reviewing the index-number problem and OBD in more detail in \cref{sec:counterfactual_OBD}, and we formalize
a form of substantive conclusion.
We give an existence proof that a real-data problem can yield two substantively different conclusions (\cref{sec:real_example}).
And we show that differences in substantive conclusions need not be driven solely by model misspecification or small sample sizes (\cref{sec:example}).
In \cref{sec:signflips}, we demonstrate that certain types of conclusion changes are common in parameter space but uncommon empirically.
We resolve this seeming disparity by observing that typical data analyses are not uniformly distributed in parameter
space --- and provide intuition for when these changes might occur (\cref{sec:no_sign_flips}). We suggest best practices for data analysts in \cref{sec:conclusion}.
