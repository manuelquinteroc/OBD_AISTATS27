\section{How common are sign flips?} 
\label{sec:signflips}

Our examples above serve as existence proofs that conclusion reversals are possible when using the OBD with different references.
In what follows, we focus on conclusions based on sign flips.
We show that sign reversals (of either the explained or unexplained component) represent a large proportion of the parameter space, in a sense we make precise below.
Then we explain why these sign flips still might not be common in practical applications.

\subsection{Signed conclusions}
\label{sec:sign_flip_characterizations}

We start by concretizing the type of decision we will focus on in our analysis. 
In particular, we will consider a conclusion based on the sign of either the explained or unexplained component of the OBD.
We will assume we are working with full population information so that a sign flip alone is enough to change conclusions;
when working with finite samples (as will always be the case in practice), we might also be concerned with statistical significance.
Although flipping from a signed value to zero would also be considered to change conclusions, we here focus on the more
extreme case where we are able to flip to the opposite sign by choosing a different reference.

\textbf{Explained component.}
Recall from \cref{eq:ob_decomp_ref_group} the two possible explained components, each corresponding to a different reference.
With this notation in hand, we can define changing a signed conclusion at the population level.
\begin{definition}
	\label{defn:sign_flip_explained}
	We say the OBD \emph{sign flips the explained component} if the sign of the explained term, $\Delta\mu^T \beta_g$, depends on the reference group. That is,
	\begin{equation}
        \label{eq:sign_flip_condition_explained_neq}
		\sign\!\left(\Delta\mu^T \beta_H\right) \neq \sign\!\left(\Delta\mu^T \beta_K\right).
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

From the definition, we can immediately see the following alternative characterization.
%
\begin{remark}
    \label{rmk:explained_ordering_sensitivity}
    The OBD sign flips the explained component if and only if the following two conditions hold:
    \begin{gather}
    	\nonumber
    	\Delta\mu^T \beta_H \neq \Delta\mu^T \beta_K \\
    \label{eq:sign_flip_condition_explained}
        \min\{\Delta\mu^T \beta_H,\ \Delta\mu^T \beta_K\}
        < 0 <
        \max\{\Delta\mu^T \beta_H,\ \Delta\mu^T \beta_K\}.
    \end{gather}
\end{remark}
Since $\Delta\mu$ is constant across analyses, sign flips in the explained component are straightforwardly driven
by differences in sign in the slope between groups.

\textbf{Unexplained component.}
Unlike the explained component, sign reversals in the unexplained component arise from the interaction between slope and intercept differences.
\begin{definition}
    \label{def:unexplained_sign_flip}
	Let $\Delta\beta := \beta_H - \beta_K$ and $\Delta\alpha := \alpha_H - \alpha_K$. The OBD \emph{sign flips the unexplained component} if the sign of the unexplained term, $\mu_{g'}^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha$, depends on the reference group. That is
	\begin{equation}
		\sign\left( \mu_K^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha \right) \neq \sign\left( \mu_H^T \Delta\beta + \Delta\alpha \right).
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The following alternative characterization for sign flips of the unexplained component is analogous to \cref{rmk:explained_ordering_sensitivity}.
%
\begin{restatable}[OBD unexplained sign flips]{proposition}{OBSignFlips}
    \label{thm:unexplained_ordering_sensitivity}
    The OBD sign flips the unexplained component if and only if the following two conditions hold:
    \begin{gather}
    	\nonumber
    	\mu_H^T \Delta\beta \neq \mu_K^T \Delta\beta \\
    \label{eq:sign_flip_condition}
   	 \min\{\mu_H^T \Delta\beta, \mu_K^T \Delta\beta\} < -\Delta\alpha < \max\{\mu_H^T \Delta\beta, \mu_K^T \Delta\beta\}.
    \end{gather}
\end{restatable}    
See \cref{sec:sign_flip_proof} for the proof.

From \cref{thm:unexplained_ordering_sensitivity}, we see that group-specific slopes serve a different role in sign flips in the unexplained case relative to the explained case.
Suppose we hold fixed the covariate means $\mu_H$ and $\mu_K$. Then we see that scaling up the magnitude of the vector change in slopes ($\Delta \beta$) induces sign flips for a greater range of values 
of the scalar change in intercepts ($\Delta \alpha$).
That is, larger magnitudes of \( \Delta \beta \) create ``more room'' for sign flips in the unexplained component.

\subsection{How many parameters lead to sign flips?}
\label{sec:sign_flip_fraction}

After characterizing sign flips in \cref{sec:sign_flip_characterizations}, we can ask how likely they are.
One formalization of this question is to ask what proportion of the parameter space corresponds to sign flips.
An immediate challenge with talking about volume within a parameter space is that the parameter space inherits
its dimension units from the covariates and response; in particular, any individual unit choice is somewhat
arbitrary (consider a choice of centimeters vs.\ millimeters for a length scale), but also different covariates need not be 
comparable (consider comparing a temperature to a weight). To alleviate this potential incompatibility between dimensions,
we first make a common standardization choice in one population: we transform the covariate means to be zero,
$\mu_K=0 \in \R^d$. How to standardize population spread and how to handle the second group are less obvious,
but we address both of these challenges by choosing $\mu_H =1\in \R^d$. \textcolor{red}{TB: what do we think about using bold or some other distinguishing notation for a 0 or 1 vector?}
We note that our standardization choice ($\mu_K=0,\mu_H=1$) is
always possible as long as $\mu_K \neq \mu_H$ (see \cref{lemma:units}), and find similar results by simulation without this standardization choice (see TODO).

In the theory that follows, we will find it convenient to restrict our focus to a bounded range of parameters. Namely, we
assume that, for some $M > 0$ and all groups $g$, the entries of $\beta_g$ and $\alpha_g$ lie in $[-M,M]$.
Importantly, we do not restrict the choice of $M$; it can be arbitrarily large. And notably, the proportions we compute in all
cases end up having no dependence on $M$.

%To do so, we must choose a region of parameter space we are interested in and then compute the volume of this region that leads to sign flips.
%What region of parameter space should we pick?
%In absence of any specific applied problem, we first standardize units of $X$ such that $\mu_K = 0$ and $\mu_H = 1$ (this is always possible as long as $\mu_K \neq \mu_H$; see \cref{lemma:units}) and then let all entries of $\beta_g$ and $\alpha_g$ lie in $[-M,M]$ for $M > 0$, $g \in \{H,K\}$.
%The fact that each $\mu_g$ has constant entries and that each entry of each $\beta_g$ lies in the same range means that we are making no assumption on the magnitude of each covariate's constribution to the response $Y$ (i.e., $\mu_{g,i}\beta_{g,i}$ sits in the same range for all $i=1,\dots,d$).
As the next two results demonstrate, under these choices, the fraction of parameters leading to sign flips is fairly large.
%
First, for the explained component, we find that, under our conditions, half of the parameter space $(\beta_H, \beta_K, \alpha_H, \alpha_K)$ corresponds to sign flips.
\begin{proposition} \label{prop:explained_fraction}
	Suppose $\mu_K = 0 \in \R^d$ and $\mu_H = 1 \in \R^d$.
	Choose any $M > 0$.
	Let $C_M \subset \R^{2d}$ be a cube with side length $2M > 0$ centered at the origin, and suppose $(\beta_H, \beta_K)$ jointly lie in this cube. Let $C_{flip} \subset C_M$ be the settings of $(\beta_H, \beta_K)$ that lead to a sign flip in the explained component.
	Then
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\mathrm{Volume}(C_{flip})}{\mathrm{Volume}(C_M)} = \frac{1}{2}.
	\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
See \cref{proof:explained_fraction} for the proof.

Second, for the unexplained component, we find that the proportion of parameter space corresponding to sign flips nears 1/2 as the dimension of the covariate vector grows.
\begin{proposition} 
\label{prop:unexplained_fraction}
	Suppose $\mu_K = 0 \in \R^d$ and $\mu_H = 1 \in \R^d$.
	Let $C_M \subset \R^{2d+2}$ be a cube with side length $2M > 0$ centered at the origin, and suppose $(\beta_K, \beta_H, \alpha_K, \alpha_H)$ jointly lie in this cube.
	Let \( I_2 \sim \text{Irwin-Hall}(2) \) and \( J_{2d} \sim \text{Irwin-Hall}(2d) \) be independent random variables.
	The fraction of $C_M$ for which a sign flip occurs in the unexplained component is given by:
\begin{align} \label{eq:unexplained_fraction}
    P_d := \mathrm{Pr} \left( \{ I_2 > 1 \} \cap \{ J_{2d} < d + 1 - I_2 \}  \right) + \nonumber \\
    \mathrm{Pr} \left( \{ I_2 < 1 \} \cap \{ J_{2d} > d + 1 - I_2 \} \right),
\end{align}
where the probability $\mathrm{Pr}$ is over the independent random variables $I_2, J_{2d}$.
Moreover, as $d \to \infty$, $P_d \rightarrow 1/2$.
\end{proposition}
See \cref{proof:unexplained_fraction} for the proof.

The joint Irwin-Hall probability in \cref{eq:unexplained_fraction} is hard to evaluate analytically, so in \cref{fig:prob_unexplained} we evaluate it numerically for various values of the dimension $d$.
We see that for even modest dimension $d$, the fraction of parameter space leading to sign flips exceeds 40\%.

\subsection{Are sign flips common in practice?}
\label{sec:sign_flips_practice}

In \cref{sec:sign_flip_fraction}, we show that a large fraction of parameter space corresponds to sign flips.
However, we here observe that empirical evidence suggests sign flips in practice may be rare.
We discuss resolutions to this apparent contradiction in \cref{sec:no_sign_flips}.

An exhaustive search of the literature applying the OBD is beyond the scope of the present paper;
particular Stata and R implementations of the OBD have hundreds of citations alone \citep{jann2008, Hlavac2022oaxaca}, 
and the paper introducing the Stata implementation has thousands of citations \citep{jann2008}.
Moreover, many papers performing the OBD do not even cite particular software implementations. \textcolor{red}{check we agree with that last claim}
Without trying to form a representative sample, we examined a selection of papers performing the OBD. 
Of the particular papers we examined, many did not report both directions of the OBD.
Of the papers that did report both directions \citep{ONeill2006, fortinLemieuxFirpo2011, sharafRashad2016, rahimiNazari2021},
none exhibited a sign flip.

Publication or other biases could affect the published literature. To avoid these concerns and also offer a more systematic investigation,
we examined collections
of OBD analyses one might potentially run on two rich, publicly available datasets. 
In each case, our systematic search for sign flips turned up few examples.
First, we examined the PhysioNet ICU cohort collected for the study of in-hospital mortality \citep{goldberger2000physiobank}.
We started by evaluating the OBD across nine predefined data subsets: the full cohort, each ICU type category, two broad age groups (patients younger than $65$ and patients aged $65$ or older), and two groups defined by a median split of the SAPS--I score. For each of these subsets, we fit gender-specific linear models and computed the OBD. We did not find any sign flips.
We then expanded our analysis to a larger and more heterogeneous collection of subpopulations.
Across all $139$ data subsets, we found that $27$ exhibited at least one sign reversal.
The HR quartile two example in \cref{sec:real_example} was one such example. We describe the data and our search procedure in more detail in 
\cref{appendix:icu_details}.

We ran a similar search on U.S.\ labor force data; in particular,
the raw data come from the 2016 American Community Survey 1-Year Public Use Microdata Sample \textcolor{red}{cite}.
We again purposely constructed a large set of data analyses to search for sign flips in different subsets of the data, comprising all combinations of the factors in Appendix \cref{tab:us_census_design}.
Out of the 355 comparisons we examined, we found 0 cases where the explained component flipped signs and 1 case where the unexplained component flipped signs.
See \cref{appendix:us_labor_force} for details of this data set and our search procedure.

We conclude from these two searches that we do not expect sign flips to be more than empirically rare, but
their existence is still possible.

\subsection{Understanding the sign-flip gap}
\label{sec:no_sign_flips}

How can it be that sign flips appear to be rare in practice (\cref{sec:sign_flips_practice}) but common in terms of parameter-space volume (\cref{sec:sign_flip_fraction})?
A key resolution is that regression parameters observed in practice need not be uniformly distributed over parameter space; in fact, we generally expect they are not.

\textbf{Explained component.} First, consider the explained component. Under the standardization we used in \cref{sec:sign_flip_fraction},
we can simplify the characterization of when a sign flip happens.
%
\begin{remark} \label{rmk:no_explained_sign_flip}
	Suppose $\mu_K = 0 \in \R^d$ and $\mu_H = 1 \in \R^d$.
	Then there is a sign flip if and only if $\sign (\sum_{i=1}^d \beta_{H,i}) \neq \sign( \sum_{i=1}^d \beta_{K,i})$.
\end{remark}
%
\textcolor{red}{TB: need to remove dangling theory in appendix that used to correspond to the remark above when it was a proposition.}
This observation follows immediately from \cref{defn:sign_flip_explained}.
In practice, we expect that the general direction of trends largely agrees, at least in sign, in each of the two groups.
For instance, consider the SBP--BMI example in \cref{sec:example}. We expect a positive relationship between BMI and SBP for both the higher-resource group $H$ and the lower-resource group $K$;
i.e., we expect $\beta_H, \beta_K > 0$.
So, by \cref{rmk:no_explained_sign_flip}, we do not expect to see a sign flip.

Similarly, \cref{rmk:no_explained_sign_flip} helps us see why few of the hospital mortality analyses (\cref{sec:sign_flips_practice} and \cref{appendix:icu_details}) show explained sign flips. A priori, we might expect that the association between any particular factor and mortality has a different magnitude for men than it does for women. But there is little a priori reason to think that any single factor might cause men's mortality to decrease but women's to increase (or vice versa).
Indeed, this observation makes the sign flip in \cref{sec:real_example} even more surprising.

\textbf{Unexplained component.} Next, consider the unexplained component. In this case, a sign flip intuitively requires the intercept and slope effects to pull in opposite directions across groups. When both move in the same direction, so that the group with higher baseline outcomes also exhibits higher returns to covariates, the unexplained component preserves its sign regardless of the chosen reference group.
We formalize these ideas in \cref{ass:no_sign_flip} and \cref{prop:no_sign_flip} next.
\begin{assumption}[Aligned intercept--slope effects]
\label{ass:no_sign_flip}
\[
    (\mu_H^T \Delta\beta)(\mu_K^T \Delta\beta) > 0 
\quad \text{and}  
\]
\[
    \sign(\Delta\alpha) = \sign(\mu_H^T \Delta\beta) = \sign(\mu_K^T \Delta\beta),
\]
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:no_sign_flip}
    Under \cref{ass:no_sign_flip}, the unexplained components 
    \( U^{(H)}\) and  \(U^{(K)}\) share the same sign.
\end{proposition}
See \cref{proof:no_sign_flip} for the proof.

\textcolor{red}{TB: seems like this area would be a good place to point to Appendix A.4 and describe a bit of the argument.}
It seems plausible that in practice we might often encounter cases where both intercept and slope advantages move in the same direction; 
\cref{prop:no_sign_flip} confirms there is no sign flip in such cases.
As an illustration, recall the gender wage gap example introduced in \cref{sec:counterfactual_example}.
\cref{ass:no_sign_flip} corresponds to settings where the group earning higher baseline wages ($\alpha_g$) also experiences higher returns to skills and education ($\beta_g$). 
When both intercept and slope advantages move in the same direction, there is no sign flip.

On the other hand, sign flips remain plausible for the unexplained component. 
Consider the SBP--BMI example in \cref{sec:example}, \cref{ass:no_sign_flip} would correspond to a case where the higher-resource group exhibits both a higher baseline SBP and a stronger SBP--BMI relationship than the lower-resource group. However, in the simulated data, the lower-resource group shows a steeper SBP--BMI slope while having a lower baseline; that is, the SBP--BMI example violates \cref{ass:no_sign_flip}, and indeed we see a sign flip in the unexplained component.
